# Prologue: The Tenement and the Boardroom

My father came home from Vietnam with a clean uniform and a habit he
could not put down. By the time I was old enough to understand what
heroin was, he was already losing. He lost in installments. He lost
his job. He lost his apartment. He lost his freedom, for a while. He
lost his immune system. He lost us, in the ways fathers who are sick
lose their children, which is slowly and then all at once.

He died of AIDS.

I tell you this on the first page of a book about coaching school
leaders because I need you to understand where the voice in these pages
is coming from. I did not come to this work from a seminar room. I came
to it from a fifth floor walk up on 110th Street in East Harlem, where
my mother raised six kids on a nurse's aide's salary and prayers that
nobody in our family prayed out loud. I came to it from a gifted and
talented program that someone, some teacher whose name my mother could
not pronounce, had put me into without asking permission. I came to it
from Cornell, where for the first three weeks I ate dinner alone
because I did not know that white kids from Long Island considered it
rude to sit at a table where you did not know anyone. I came to it
from a classroom in the South Bronx, and then from the principal's
office of a school I built with eleven other people, and eventually
from a conference room at Columbia Business School where I teach
executives, now, how to coach somebody.

The distance between that tenement and that conference room is, on a
map, about ninety blocks. In every other way it is the distance this
book is trying to measure.

## September 2003

The building at 360 East 145th Street did not want us.

It had belonged, for years, to a junior high that the city had finally
closed because the violence inside it had become a public embarrassment
even by the forgiving standards of 1990s New York. When we got the
keys, in the late summer of 2003, the lockers still had names scratched
into them, names of children who by then were adults, who by then in
many cases were in Rikers or in the ground. The cafeteria smelled like
bleach that had been asked to do too much. The windows on the fourth
floor did not open. The elevator did not work. There was a spot on the
stairwell wall, between the second and third floors, where somebody had
once been stabbed, and you could still see, if you looked, where the
paint had been touched up.

This was the school we had chosen.

We had chosen it because the children of Mott Haven deserved a school
in their own neighborhood, and because none of the charter operators
who had been promised this zip code by the reform movement had actually
wanted to plant their flag on a block this rough. We had chosen it
because I had grown up eleven subway stops away, and I knew what it
meant to a kid to have a building that took itself seriously. We had
chosen it because a group of eleven educators, whose names are on the
dedication page, had sat with me in a borrowed conference room on 65th
Street for six months and built a school on butcher paper.

We had surveyed the neighborhood kids, because nobody else had. We
asked them, on paper, what kind of high school they would want to come
to if they got to design one. Two words kept coming back. They wanted
*finance*, because they had watched their mothers pay bills and they
knew, in the way children know, that money was where the grown world
decided who got to breathe. And they wanted *technology*, because by
2003 even a child in the South Bronx could see that the future was
going to be typed, not written. So we named the school The Laboratory
School of Finance and Technology. We called it a laboratory on purpose.
We were going to try things. Some of them were going to fail.

On the first morning, I arrived at six forty, unlocked the door, and
sat on the front steps with a paper cup of coffee from the bodega on
the corner. The owner, Don Luis, had given it to me for free. He said,
*you're the principal now, we take care of you*. I am fifty-seven years
old and I still remember the weight of that cup in my hand.

## What coaching has to do with any of this

I built a school. This book is not about how to build a school. Plenty
of people have written that book, and some of them were more
systematic about it than I was.

This book is about the thing I did not know I was going to have to do,
which was build *principals*. Over the eighteen years I ran MS 223 I
trained, mentored, coached, and eventually sent out into the world
more than a hundred people who are now running schools themselves.
Some of them run schools in the Bronx. Some of them run schools in
Yonkers and Denver and Boston. Two of them run schools on three other
continents. Every single one of them sat in my office, at some point,
and cried. Not because I made them cry, although I have occasionally
been accused of it. They cried because the job is that hard, and
because once you care about children this much, your nervous system
does not know where to put all of it.

Coaching, it turns out, is where you put it.

Coaching is the practice by which one human being helps another human
being become more capable of doing hard work without being destroyed by
it. That definition is mine. You will find fancier ones in the chapters
that follow. Bloom and Wilson, who invented the framework called
Blended Coaching, will tell you it is the integration of facilitative
and instructional support in service of professional growth (Bloom and
Wilson, 2023). Costa and Garmston, whose Cognitive Coaching is the
gentlest and most demanding method I have ever used, will tell you it
is the mediated development of self-directed practitioners (Costa and
Garmston, 2015). The RAND Corporation, which ran two randomized
controlled trials of principal coaching and published the results in
2020, will tell you it is an intervention with a measurable dosage and
proximity effect on student achievement in English Language Arts
(Herman et al., 2020). They are all correct.

They are also all leaving something out.

What they are leaving out is the part where a forty one year old woman
in her first year as a principal sits across a table from you and says,
*I don't know if I can keep doing this*, and you do not say *here are
five strategies for managing overwhelm*, because anybody can say that.
What you say, instead, is the thing a coach says. Which is usually a
question. Which is sometimes silence. Which is occasionally *let's
look at the data together, because I think you are telling yourself a
story that is not true.*

Coaching is, in that sense, an act of love that wears professional
clothes. I am a practitioner and a scholar and the founder of an
executive coaching service at Columbia Business School, and I am
telling you on page six that coaching is an act of love. If that
sentence makes you uncomfortable, this may not be the book for you. If
it does not, keep going.

## Who this book is for

This book is for the principal who is sitting in her car in the
parking lot at six forty seven in the morning, watching the children
walk past her windshield, and wondering if she has it in her to get
out of the car today.

This book is for the superintendent who has been in the job eight
months and has realized, with a private horror he has not yet told
anyone about, that there is nobody in the district he can safely tell
the truth to.

This book is for the assistant principal who has been coached well and
knows it, who wants to pay it forward, who is trying to figure out
whether to sign up for the certification program at Columbia or the one
at Teachers College or the one the state is running for free.

This book is for the district leader who is about to spend six hundred
thousand dollars on principal professional development and has a
nagging suspicion that the last six hundred thousand she spent did not
move a single number.

This book is for my former teachers, who are now principals themselves,
and who still email me on a Sunday night at nine thirty with a question
that could have waited until Monday but did not.

And this book is for the boy or girl somewhere in a fifth floor walk
up right now who does not yet know that someone is going to put them
in a gifted and talented program without asking, and change everything.

## A map of the journey

I do not like the word *journey*. Every time an educator uses the word
*journey* I assume I am about to be sold something. So let me call
this, instead, a map.

Part One tells the truth about the job. The job has changed. The
preparation has not. The principalship that your own principal did in
1987, and the principalship I am describing to you now in 2026, are
not the same work. They share a title and nothing else. Part One is
three chapters on what the work has become, why the people doing it
are lonely, and what we owe the children who are waiting for us to
figure this out.

Part Two is the toolkit. Six coaching traditions, each of them with a
chapter. I chose these six because they are the ones I have actually
used, in my own office, on actual human beings, with observable
effects. Blended Coaching, from Gary Bloom and Jackie Wilson, which is
the one I reach for when a new principal needs both a hug and a
syllabus. Cognitive Coaching, from Art Costa and Bob Garmston, which
is the one I reach for when a veteran principal has gotten too clever
and needs to remember how to think. The Executive Development Program,
from the National Institute for School Leadership, which the RAND
Corporation evaluated in the single most important randomized
controlled trial ever conducted on principal coaching, and which gave
us the first clean causal evidence that this work, done correctly,
moves children. The ISAL cohort model, from the Illinois Association
of School Administrators, which is the closest thing I have seen to a
cure for superintendent loneliness. Emotional Intelligence and
Situational Leadership, which is not really one framework but two,
stitched together by anyone who has actually had to tell a veteran
teacher that her practice is hurting children. And the Leadership
Performance Planning Worksheet, from the NYC Leadership Academy, which
gave a generation of New York City principals, including me, a shared
language for the work that had previously been every principal for
himself.

Part Three is the evidence. What we know about coaching and burnout.
What we know about coaching and equity. What we know about coaching
and student achievement, and what the honest chain of reasoning from
a coach in a conference room to a child in a classroom actually looks
like.

Part Four is the playbook. If you run a district, this is where I tell
you what to do on Monday.

There is also an Epilogue, which is about the last morning I was the
principal of MS 223, and a set of Appendices, which are the reference
cards I wish I had had on my first day.

## One promise

I am going to tell you hard things in this book. I am going to tell
you that most principal professional development does not work. I am
going to tell you that most of what passes for coaching in American
school districts is not coaching, it is surveillance with a friendlier
tone. I am going to tell you that we are losing good principals,
especially principals of color, at a rate that should embarrass us,
and that the coaching apparatus we have built to retain them has
mostly failed. I am going to tell you that the research on our side
is thinner than we pretend it is, and that the research on the side
of the people who want to close our schools is not as strong as they
pretend it is either.

I am going to tell you all of that because I love this work, and
because I believe that love without honesty is a form of cowardice.

But I am also going to tell you, at every step, what has worked. I am
going to tell you about the assistant principal in her first year who
learned to give real feedback to a teacher twice her age, because her
coach taught her how. I am going to tell you about the superintendent
in Colorado who almost quit in October, and who is now coaching other
superintendents in her state. I am going to tell you about the math
department at MS 223 that won a national award because a coach I will
not name helped three teachers see what they were missing. I am going
to tell you about the parents in Mott Haven who organized a lending
library in a bodega on Willis Avenue because we asked them to, and
what that library taught me about what coaching actually is.

The work is hard. The children are waiting. We have, all of us,
less time than we think.

Let's begin.
