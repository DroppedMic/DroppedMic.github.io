# Prologue: The Tenement and the Boardroom

My father came home from Vietnam with a clean uniform and a habit he
could not put down.

By the time I was old enough to understand what heroin was, he was
already losing. He lost his job. Then his freedom, for a while. Then
his immune system. The way fathers who are sick lose their children,
which is slowly and then all at once.

He died of AIDS.

I tell you that on the first page of a book about coaching school
leaders because I need you to know where the voice in these pages is
coming from. I did not come to this work through a seminar room. I
came up through a tenement in East Harlem, six kids and a mother who
did not have the luxury of tired. A teacher put me into a gifted and
talented program when I was a boy, without asking permission. That
teacher changed my life, and I cannot remember her name. There is a
scholarship that took me to Cornell, a master's at City College, and
then two more degrees from Teachers College at Columbia. There is a
classroom, eventually, in the South Bronx. There is the school I
built with eleven other people in 2003. And there is, now, a
conference room at Columbia Business School, where I teach people
who run companies how to coach the leaders under them.

The distance between the tenement and the boardroom is the distance
this book is trying to measure.

## September 2003

The building did not want us.

It had belonged, for years, to a junior high that New York City had
finally closed because what went on inside it had become a public
embarrassment even by the standards of the 1990s. The neighborhood
had been promised, more than once, that somebody was going to come in
and open something better. The charter operators who were collecting
most of the reform money in those years had looked at Mott Haven and
decided their flag would plant easier somewhere else. So we walked in.

Eleven other people and I walked in.

We had met for months in borrowed rooms around the city, building a
school on butcher paper. We had not been told we were allowed to do
that. We just did it, because a group of educators who care about
children can usually outwork a bureaucracy if they stay quiet about
it long enough. One thing we did that nobody else had thought to do
was ask the kids. We handed out surveys in the neighborhood. We asked
them what they would want a school to be, if a school were allowed
to be what they wanted.

Two words came back. *Finance*, because they had watched their
mothers count bills on the kitchen table, and they knew, in the
particular way children in the Bronx know, that money was where the
grown world decided whose child got to breathe. And *technology*,
because by 2003 even a twelve year old in Mott Haven could see that
the future was going to be typed rather than written. So we named
the school The Laboratory School of Finance and Technology. We called
it a laboratory on purpose. We were going to try things. Some of
them were going to fail. We would put the failures in a filing
cabinet and open the cabinet when new people came to work for us, so
they would know the building had a sense of humor about itself.

That is how we started.

## Our kids

In the Smithsonian profile that came out a few years later, a
reporter wrote down a thing I had said to her, and I am not going to
pretend I do not still think about it every time I walk past a school
in the Bronx. I told her, about our children, that *every day they
walk into this building, they are taking a stand against poverty*
(*Smithsonian Magazine*, 2009). Ninety percent of them qualified for
free lunch. Fifteen percent were living in a shelter that year.
Twenty percent were still learning English. I called them *my kids*
then and I call them *our kids* now, because that is what it was and
is.

I grew up with neighbors in Harlem who were, in my mother's phrase,
our *community cousins*, Puerto Rican and Black and Italian American.
That is the neighborhood I was trying to build inside a building,
eleven subway stops south of where I grew up. I was not trying to
reinvent schooling. I was trying to reinvent the feeling of having a
family, for children who were not always getting that feeling from
anywhere else.

Reasonable people can disagree about whether a school should try to
do that. I will not pretend to be neutral. I think it is the job.

## What coaching has to do with any of this

I built a school. This book is not about building a school. Plenty
of people have written that book, and some of them were more
organized about it than I was.

This book is about the thing I did not know I was going to have to
do, which was build *principals*. Over eighteen years at MS 223 I
trained and mentored and eventually sent out into the world more
than a hundred people who run schools now. Some of them run schools
in New York. Some run schools in Boston and Denver and Yonkers. Some
of them, through the work I do abroad, run schools in cities I could
not have found on a map when I was their age. Almost every one of
them, at one point or another, sat in my office and cried. They
cried because the work is that hard. They cried because once you
love children this much, your nervous system does not know where to
put all of it.

Coaching, it turns out, is where you put it.

Coaching is the practice by which one person helps another person
become more capable of doing hard work without being destroyed by it.
That definition is mine. You will find more careful ones in the
chapters that follow. Bloom and Wilson, who gave us Blended Coaching,
will tell you it is the integration of facilitative and instructional
support in service of a practitioner's growth (Bloom & Wilson, 2023).
Costa and Garmston, whose Cognitive Coaching is the gentlest and the
most demanding method I have ever used, will tell you it is the
mediated development of a self directed learner (Costa & Garmston,
2015). The RAND Corporation, which ran two randomized controlled
trials of principal coaching and published the results in 2020, will
tell you it is an intervention whose effect on student achievement
depends on dosage, proximity, and alignment (Herman et al., 2020).
They are all correct.

They are also, every one of them, leaving something out.

What they leave out is the part where a principal in her first year
sits across a table from you and says, *I do not know if I can keep
doing this*, and you do not respond with five strategies for managing
overwhelm, because any consultant with a slide deck can offer five
strategies for managing overwhelm. What you do instead is the thing a
coach does. Which is usually a question. Which is sometimes silence.
Which is occasionally, *let us look at the data together, because I
think you are telling yourself a story that is not true*.

Coaching, in that sense, is an act of love that happens to wear
professional clothes. I am a practitioner and a scholar and the
founder of an executive coaching service at Columbia Business School,
and I am telling you on page six that coaching is an act of love.
That sentence is going to make some readers close this book. If you
are still with me, keep going.

## Who this book is for

For the principal who is sitting in her car in the school parking lot
a few minutes before seven in the morning, watching children walk past
the windshield, wondering if she has it in her to get out of the car
today.

For the superintendent eight months into the job who has realized,
with a private horror he has not yet told anyone, that there is
nobody in his district he can safely tell the truth to.

For the assistant principal trying to decide whether to sign up for
the certification at Columbia, or the one at Teachers College, or
the one the state is running on a grant that nobody is sure will
renew.

For the district leader who is about to approve a half million
dollar contract for principal professional development, and has a
quiet suspicion, one she has not shared in any of the meetings, that
the last contract did not move a single number she actually cares
about.

For the teachers I trained who are running schools now, and who
still reach me at odd hours with questions that could have waited
until Monday, except they could not.

And for the boy or the girl in a fifth floor walk up somewhere in
this country, right now, who does not yet know that someone is
about to put them in a gifted program without asking, and change
everything.

## A map

I am not going to call this a journey. Every time an educator uses
the word *journey* I assume I am being sold something. Call it a
map.

Part One is the honest description of what the job has become. The
principalship your own principal ran in 1987 and the principalship I
am describing in 2026 share a title, and very little else. Part One
is three chapters on what the work has turned into, why the people
doing it are lonely, and what we owe the children who are waiting
for us to figure it out.

Part Two is the toolkit. Six coaching traditions, each with a
chapter, each a method I have used on real people with observable
effects. Blended Coaching, from Gary Bloom and Jackie Wilson, the
one I reach for when a new principal needs both a hug and a
syllabus. Cognitive Coaching, from Art Costa and Bob Garmston, the
one I reach for when a veteran has gotten too clever and needs to
remember how to think. The Executive Development Program, from the
National Institute for School Leadership, whose RAND evaluation is
the cleanest causal evidence we have that this work, done right,
moves children (Herman et al., 2020). The ISAL cohort model, from
the Illinois Association of School Administrators, the closest thing
I have seen to a cure for the particular loneliness of the
superintendency. Emotional Intelligence and Situational Leadership,
two traditions stitched together by anyone who has ever had to tell
a veteran teacher that her practice is hurting children. And the
Leadership Performance Planning Worksheet, from the NYC Leadership
Academy, which gave a generation of New York City principals a
shared language for work that had been, until then, every principal
for himself (NYC Leadership Academy, 2018).

Part Three is the evidence. What we know about coaching and
burnout. What we know about coaching and equity. What we know about
coaching and student achievement, and what the honest chain of
reasoning from a coach in a conference room to a child in a
classroom actually looks like.

Part Four is the playbook. If you run a district, this is where I
tell you what to do on Monday.

The Epilogue is about a last morning, which I will not describe
here, and the Appendices are the reference cards I wish I had had
on my first day.

## One promise

I will tell you hard things in this book.

Most principal professional development does not work. Most of what
passes for coaching in American school districts is not coaching at
all, it is surveillance with a friendlier tone. We are losing good
principals, especially principals of color, at a rate that should
embarrass us, and the coaching apparatus we built to retain them
has, in most districts, failed. The research on our side is thinner
than we pretend. The research on the other side, the side that
wants to close our schools and replace them, is also thinner than
they pretend. I am going to tell you all of that because I love
this work, and because love without honesty is a form of cowardice.

I will also tell you what has worked.

An assistant principal in her first year, too young and too junior
to be taken seriously by the faculty, who learned under coaching
how to give real feedback to a veteran who had been teaching longer
than she had been alive. A superintendent who almost quit in
October of her second year, who finished the year anyway, and the
one after it, and is coaching her peers now. The math department
at MS 223, which was named a national Intel School of Distinction
in 2010 because three teachers did something in their classrooms
that the rest of us could not stop watching (*MS/HS 223 Archive,*
2010). The parents in Mott Haven who turned a bodega into a lending
library, the first lending library in a bodega in the history of
the South Bronx as far as anyone I have asked can remember, because
someone on our staff had the idea and the courage to walk into the
store and ask. The work is hard. The children are waiting. All of
us have less time than we think.

Let us begin.
