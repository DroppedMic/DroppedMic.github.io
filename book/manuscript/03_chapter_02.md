# Chapter 2: Alone on the Second Floor

The light in the principal's office is on at 7:14 in the evening.

She has been there since 6:30 in the morning. The custodial crew is
finishing up two flights down, sweeping the cafeteria, the radio on
their belt running low on battery and chirping out a song from the
1990s in a static she can almost hum along to. Her phone has a notification
she has not opened, from her sister, because she knows what it says
and she cannot, this evening, be the person who answers it. The desk
is covered with: a discipline referral she has not signed, a parent
letter she has rewritten three times, a stack of teacher observation
forms she promised the assistant superintendent on Friday, and the
remains of a yogurt that was her lunch.

She has been the principal for two and a half years. She is good at
it. Tonight, she is also lonely in a way that does not have a
vocabulary inside the school building, and she does not know who, in
the world she still has access to, she could call about it without
sounding like a person who is doing badly.

This is the chapter about that.

## The structural loneliness of the principalship

Every job has its private griefs. The principalship has one that is
specific and underdiscussed, and it is this. The principal is the
only person in the building whose role forbids most of the available
forms of intimacy at work.

She cannot be friends, in the ordinary sense, with the teachers she
supervises. She can be warm. She can be loyal. She can be, on a good
day, beloved. She cannot, however, sit down at the staff lunch table
and complain, the way the rest of the staff complains, about the
person who runs the building, because she is the person who runs the
building. She cannot tell the assistant principal, in confidence,
that she is worried she is not up to the job, because the assistant
principal is auditioning, whether she knows it or not, to replace
her.

She cannot be friends, in any straightforward way, with the parents,
because the parents, in any given week, are the people whose
children she has just suspended or whose teacher she has just
defended or whose enrichment program she has just cut for budget
reasons. She cannot be friends with the superintendent's office,
because the superintendent's office signs her evaluation. She cannot
be friends with the union representative, because the union
representative is the person she will be sitting across from the
next time a grievance gets filed.

This leaves: her family, her friends from before this job, and the
other principals in her district. Her family, much of the time, does
not understand what she does. Her friends from before this job
slowly stop calling, because she stopped having time to return their
messages somewhere in year two. The other principals in her district
are the people the most plausibly available to her, and they are
also her direct competitors for resources, position, and the
attention of the central office. She is, in the structural sense
that an org chart can show you, alone on the second floor.

I am not making a poetic point. CASEL, which is not an organization
that goes in for emotional excess, published a 2023 piece titled
*Leadership in a Loneliness Epidemic: School Leaders Are No Exception
to Isolation* (CASEL, 2023). The phrase *epidemic* is not mine. It is
theirs. They use it because the literature on workplace loneliness has
caught up to what those of us inside the work have known for years,
which is that the principalship is structured, almost as if by
deliberate design, to exclude its occupant from most of the
relationships available to everyone else under her supervision.

Most principals who burn out do not burn out from the workload. The
workload is high but it is not, by itself, what destroys you. What
destroys you is doing high workload work in a structural condition
of loneliness, week after week, year after year, with nobody to tell
the truth to.

## Why nobody talks about it

I will offer a personal observation, which is that principals,
particularly women principals and particularly principals of color,
are rewarded throughout their careers for a quality I will call,
without irony, *competence performance*. We were good students. We
were good teachers. We were good assistant principals. We learned
early that the way to advance in this profession was to be the
person at the table who looked, at all times, like she had it
together.

The job we trained for, in other words, selected for the people
least likely to admit when they did not.

This is not a moral failing. It is a selection effect. If you spent
twenty years getting promoted on the strength of your composure, you
do not, on the day you finally get the office on the second floor,
suddenly acquire the language to walk into someone else's office and
say, *I am not okay this week.*

So the loneliness compounds. The principal who is doing well
publicly is, often, doing badly privately, and the strongest evidence
that she is doing badly privately is the absence of any place where
she could safely say so.

This is the gap into which coaching, when it works, steps.

## Why superintendents have it worse

The principal is alone on the second floor. The superintendent is
alone in the entire district.

The numbers, briefly, because I want you to see the shape of the
problem. The American Association of School Administrators reported
in its 2025 mid decade update on the superintendency that the
average current tenure is 5.4 years (AASA, 2025). Two thirds of
superintendents reported at least considerable stress in their role,
and stress management was the second most requested area of growth
they named for themselves, behind only finance and budget planning
(AASA, 2025). Among the largest five hundred districts in the
country, turnover in 2023 to 2024 ran at 23 percent.

A superintendent of a midsize district, on any given Tuesday, has no
peer in her own district. There is no other superintendent in the
building, because there is no other superintendent in the
organization. Her direct reports are her cabinet, who are auditioning
for her job in the same way her assistant principal was auditioning
for hers. Her supervisors are the school board, whose membership
turns over with every election cycle and whose loyalties are
political rather than professional. The other superintendents in her
state are her peers in the abstract sense, but in practice they are
people she sees four times a year at a conference and competes with
for headlines.

A superintendent who tells the truth, out loud, in any of those
rooms, is a superintendent making a decision that will follow her for
the rest of her career. So most of them do not.

This is why the cohort model that the Illinois Association of School
Administrators built into its School for Advanced Leadership is, in
my view, the closest thing the field has produced to a structural
answer to the problem (IASA, 2025). I will spend an entire chapter
on ISAL later in the book. The reason it works, when it works, is
that it engineers a recurring container in which superintendents can
say what they cannot say in their own districts, to people who have
no political reason to misuse the information. That is a hard thing
to engineer. The Illinois group, by my count, is one of about three
or four organizations in the country that has done it well.

## Coaching as the safety net

Here is the argument I have been building toward in this chapter.

The principalship and the superintendency, as they are structured in
American public education, manufacture loneliness as a side product
of the role. You cannot redesign your way out of the loneliness
without redesigning the role itself, which would require collective
bargaining changes, evaluation reform, and, in many districts, a
change in the political relationship between the school board and
the executive. None of that is going to happen on the timeline of any
individual leader's tenure.

What you can do, and what coaching is for, is build a sanctioned
relationship that sits adjacent to the role and gives the leader a
person to whom she can say true things without consequence.

That is the safety net argument for coaching, and I want to be
careful with it, because the safety net argument is also the argument
most often used to justify versions of coaching that are, in
practice, not coaching at all. A leader does not need a person to
vent to. She can vent to her sister. A leader does not need a friend.
She has friends, however far underused. What she needs is a person
who is professionally trained to listen to the work, who has sat in
the chair she is sitting in, and who will, at the moment when she
needs it most, ask her the question she does not want to answer.

The safety net is not catching her. The safety net is asking her,
*are you sure you want to be falling right now? Because I think,
maybe, you are jumping.*

A coach who only catches is a friend with a fee schedule. A coach
who only confronts is a supervisor in disguise. The art is in the
calibration, and Part Two of this book is essentially six chapters
on six different traditions of how that calibration has been worked
out by people more careful than I am.

## What coaching cannot do

I want to be honest, again, about the limits.

Coaching does not cure loneliness. It can address the structural
loneliness of the role. It cannot address the human loneliness of a
human being. A leader who is lonely in the rest of her life will
find that coaching does not, by itself, fix that, and a coach who
pretends otherwise is doing her a disservice. There is a chapter on
this later (Chapter 10) where I will talk about the relationship
between coaching, therapy, and friendship, and where I will be very
clear about which problems belong to which intervention.

Coaching also does not protect a leader from the consequences of bad
decisions. I have heard from principals, more than once, who were
surprised that their coach did not protect them from a critical
performance review. The coach is not, in any sense, the leader's
advocate inside the bureaucracy. The coach is the person who, the
week before the bad review, would have been telling the leader the
things she was not yet ready to hear about why the review was
coming. If the leader did not listen, that is on the leader. The
coach is not, and should not be, an extension of the leader's
political project.

Coaching, finally, does not scale by itself. One coached principal in
a district of fifty principals is a humanitarian intervention. It is
not a strategy. The leaders I most worry about, in fact, are the
ones who get a coach as a perk of being identified as high
potential, and who then operate in a building and a district that is
otherwise hostile to the kind of slow, reflective work the coach is
trying to support. Coaching at scale, which is what Part Four of
this book is about, is a structural commitment by a district. One
off coaching is, with the best of intentions, a band aid.

But a band aid, on the right cut, can keep a person alive long
enough to get to a hospital. I have watched a single coaching
relationship save a career more than once. I will not pretend that
is nothing.

> **Use Case · The Bathroom Cry**
>
> *A composite, drawn from the experience of multiple principals the
> author has coached during his career as a principal supervisor and
> at Boston University. Names, places, and identifying details are
> changed. Nothing in this account is fabricated; all of it is
> disguised.*
>
> She locked herself in the women's bathroom on the second floor
> between fourth and fifth period.
>
> She had been crying, quietly, for about three minutes, and she had
> approximately seven more minutes before the bell, the change of
> classes, and the fact that she was, in addition to being the
> principal, the only adult who could get the autistic student in
> Room 217 to transition without a meltdown. She splashed water on
> her face. She redid her eye makeup with a toner pad and a prayer.
> She walked out of the bathroom, smiled at a sixth grader who was
> heading to her locker, and got to Room 217 in time.
>
> Nobody saw her cry. That was the point.
>
> She told me about it on a Thursday call, six weeks later, because I
> had asked her, in passing, whether she had cried at work recently,
> and she had said yes, and I had said, *tell me about that.*
>
> What had set her off was a conversation in the hallway, about
> twenty seconds long, with a teacher she liked. The teacher had
> stopped her on the way to the office and said, with no particular
> heat, *you know, you used to be more available before you took this
> job.* The teacher had meant it kindly. The teacher had been her
> friend, before the principalship.
>
> The principal had said something polite back, walked into the
> stairwell, made it to the second floor bathroom, and shut the door.
>
> What she could not say to the teacher, what she could not say to
> her assistant principal, what she had not said to her husband
> because she did not want him to suggest she quit, was that the
> teacher was right. She was not as available. She was not as warm.
> She was not, in the relational sense, as much herself as she had
> been before. The job had reorganized her, and the reorganization
> was costing her relationships she had cherished, and there was no
> mechanism inside her week for naming that cost out loud.
>
> *Until*, she said on the call, *you asked me if I had cried at work
> recently. And I realized I had not told anyone.*
>
> What we did, on that Thursday call, was not strategy. It was not a
> five point plan. We named the cost. We named that the cost was real
> and that it was not, at the same time, a sign she was failing.
> Plenty of competent principals lose plenty of friendships. The
> question was not whether the cost was happening. The question was
> what she wanted to do with the costs she could see.
>
> Over the next year, she made three changes. She rebuilt one of the
> friendships, on purpose, with a former colleague who had also moved
> into administration. She started a monthly dinner with two
> principals from neighboring districts, the three of them eating
> badly cooked Italian food and saying things to each other they
> could not say in their own buildings. And she stopped, hard, the
> habit of using her assistant principal as the person she vented to,
> because she had finally accepted that she could not have it both
> ways with that relationship.
>
> She did not cry in the bathroom again that year, as far as she
> told me. I do not believe she will tell me about it the next time
> it happens. The next time it happens, though, she will, I hope,
> have somewhere to go with the feeling that is not a stall and a
> mirror and a toner pad.
>
> What her district should have done, before she ever got to the
> bathroom, is the subject of Chapter 14. The short version is that
> her district should have built her a coaching relationship in her
> first year, structured her cohort so that she had peers she could
> safely talk to, and stopped using her assistant principal as the
> de facto succession plan that made honesty between them
> impossible. None of that is exotic. All of it is what a district
> that is serious about retaining principals does.
>
> Most districts are not serious about retaining principals. They
> are serious about hiring them, and replacing them, and writing
> press releases when one of them quits. The difference between the
> first set of verbs and the second set is mostly a coaching
> infrastructure. We will get there in Part Four.
