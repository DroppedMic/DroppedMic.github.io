# Chapter 3: What Our Kids Deserve From Us

A child in our building, in any given year, was statistically likely
to be living in one of three conditions.

She was likely to be in a household where the adult income, divided
across the people relying on it, fell below the federal threshold
that qualified her, automatically, for free school lunch. Ninety
percent of our students did. She was somewhat likely, in any given
year, to be living in a homeless shelter. About fifteen percent
were. She was likely, if she was newly arrived to the country, to be
in the early stages of acquiring English as a second or third
language. About twenty percent of our population fit that description
(*Smithsonian Magazine,* 2009).

Those numbers describe a school. They do not describe a child. A
child is a particular person with a name and a backpack and a
favorite sandwich and a story she tells about her grandmother. The
numbers tell you the conditions she is walking into school under.
They do not tell you who she is.

I wrote this chapter, third in the book, because I think the
conditions and the child are the two things every leader has to be
able to hold in mind at the same time. If you can only hold the
numbers, you become a technocrat with a heart of paper. If you can
only hold the child, you become a sentimentalist with a faculty
meeting that runs forty minutes long and accomplishes nothing. The
work, the actual work of running a school in a neighborhood like
ours, requires you to know both, in the same minute, without
flinching at either.

## What that line actually means

I told a reporter from *Smithsonian*, in 2009, that *every day they
walk into this building, they are taking a stand against poverty*
(*Smithsonian Magazine,* 2009). I have repeated the line many times
since. I have also, in private, with people I trust, complained
about it.

I complain about it because the line, when it leaves my mouth, can
do two different things. It can describe a fact, which is what I
meant when I said it the first time, or it can do the work of a
slogan, which is what other people have, occasionally, used it for.

The fact is this. A child who shows up to a public middle school in
the South Bronx, on time, in the school uniform, having eaten or
not eaten breakfast, having slept or not slept the night before, is
performing, by the act of showing up, an assertion that her future
is not going to be determined entirely by the conditions she walked
in under. The school, by being open, by being staffed, by being
ready for her, is co signing that assertion. That is what the line
means. It is a description of what the building, on a working day,
is for.

The slogan version of the line is the version that is printed on a
banner in the lobby, said at the assembly on the first day of
school, and then promptly forgotten by the adults responsible for
acting on it. The slogan version is, at best, decorative. At worst,
it is an alibi. If you have the line on the banner and you do not,
on a Tuesday morning at ten thirty, walk down to the third floor
and personally retrieve a child from a classroom where she is being
spoken to by an adult in a tone that does not match the line on the
banner, then the line is not doing the work it claims to be doing.
It is, in that case, an exhibit, not a commitment.

A principal's job, in part, is to keep the line from becoming an
exhibit. That is harder than it sounds.

## Equity as the starting line

I want to be careful with the word *equity*. The word has been used,
over the past decade, in so many different ways by so many different
people that it is not always clear what is meant when an educator
says it. I am going to tell you what I mean.

Equity, in this book, means that a child's chances of acquiring the
skills, dispositions, and credentials necessary to choose her own
adult life are not, in practice, predicted by the income, language,
race, or housing status of the household she was born into. That is
my definition. It is not the only good one. It is the one that
governs everything else I am going to argue.

Equity in that sense is the starting line, not the finish line. A
school that is committed to equity is committed to ensuring that the
race begins at the same place for every child. It is not, and
cannot, by itself, guarantee where any individual child finishes.
The finish is determined by many things, most of them outside any
school's control. The starting line is determined by the school. If
a school is failing to deliver an equitable starting line, no
sentimental story about an exceptional graduate excuses the failure
for the rest of the children that school is supposed to serve.

I have heard, over the years, many principals tell me about the
exceptional kid who graduated from their school and went on to do
something impressive. I am happy for the kid. I am, often, suspicious
of the principal. The exceptional kid is the kid who was going to
make it under almost any conditions. The honest measure of a school
is what happened to the average kid, the median kid, the kid in the
twentieth percentile, the kid whose name nobody at the press
conference will remember.

A school committed to equity organizes itself around the average
kid. Not around the exception. Not around the failure. Around the
kid in the middle of the distribution, who is the kid whose
trajectory most depends on whether the institution she walked into
was prepared to do its job.

That is the kid the principal is the guarantor of.

## The principal as guarantor

The word *guarantor* is not a word I picked up in graduate school.
It is a word I picked up from parents.

Over the years, more than one father and more than one mother sat
across from me and said, in different ways, in English and in
Spanish, the same thing. They said they needed to know whether the
school was going to be the institution that guaranteed their child's
chances, because if the school was not, then they were, and they
were running out of strength. *El garante de las oportunidades de mi
hija.* The guarantor of my daughter's chances. I have used the
phrase to describe my job to many people, including most of the
principals I have coached, because no phrase I have heard in any
leadership course I have taken or taught gets at the work as well
as those parents got at it sitting across from me in their work
clothes.

Being the guarantor of a child's chances does not mean guaranteeing
her success. It means guaranteeing the conditions under which her
success becomes possible. It means: there will be a teacher in the
classroom every day, and that teacher will be qualified, and that
teacher will be supported in being good at her job, and the
classroom itself will be safe, and clean, and quiet enough to
think in, and the curriculum will be honest, and the discipline
system will be fair, and the cafeteria will serve food a child can
actually eat, and the office will know your name when you walk in,
and the principal will pick up the phone when you call.

That is what guaranteeing the chances looks like. It is not
glamorous. It is mostly the absence of the failures that other
buildings, in the same district, with the same kids, are routinely
allowing.

I am the guarantor or I am not. Most days I am. Some days I am not,
and those are the days I have learned to ask for help.

## Why this requires coaching

Equity centered leadership, as the Wallace Foundation called it in a
2023 report whose framing I want to borrow, is not built through
mission statements (Wallace Foundation, 2023). It is built through
the slow accumulation of small, hard decisions, made under pressure,
by leaders who have been trained to recognize the equity stakes of
decisions that look, on their surface, like operational decisions.

A principal decides, on a Wednesday in October, which two students
to refer to the new gifted and talented pull out program. There are
six children who could plausibly qualify. She has two slots. The two
she picks will, almost certainly, be enrolled in advanced classes for
the rest of their middle school years. The four she does not pick
will, almost certainly, not be. The principal knows this, in
principle. In the moment of deciding, she goes with the two children
whose parents have, all year, been most active in the school. Both
of those children are, as it happens, white. The four she does not
pick are children of color whose parents work two jobs and have not
been able to come to a single after school event.

The principal did not, in any conscious sense, make a racist
decision. She made, under time pressure, a decision that her habits,
her relationships, and the demographics of her parent volunteer
network nudged her into. The decision was racist in effect. It will
shape four childhoods for the worse and two childhoods for the
better, and the principal will not, without help, ever notice that
this is what happened.

This is the work coaching does, on the equity side, that no
workshop on its own can do. A coach who is sitting with that
principal, three weeks later, asking her to walk through her gifted
and talented referrals and her reasoning, will catch the pattern.
A coach who has been trained, specifically, to notice the equity
implications of decisions that present themselves as operational
will name what the principal could not, in the moment, see. Not as
accusation. As mirror. *Here is what I noticed. Here is the pattern.
Here is the question I want to leave with you between now and next
week.*

I am going to spend a chapter, later in the book, on the specific
practices a coaching relationship uses to build equity centered
leadership over time (Chapter 11). For now, I want to plant the
flag. Equity, in a school, is the cumulative product of many small
decisions made by leaders under pressure. Coaching is the
intervention best designed to interrupt the pressure, surface the
patterns, and give the leader a second chance to choose. There is no
shortcut. There is, in particular, no shortcut that consists of
posting the word *equity* on a banner in the main hallway and
expecting the work to be done by gravity.

> **Use Case · The Bodega Library**
>
> *Drawn from the Community Reading Initiative the author developed
> at MS 223 with parents and local merchants in Mott Haven. The
> Initiative and its bodega lending libraries are real and a matter
> of public record (FCNY, n.d.). The shopkeepers and parents
> involved are not named here in order to protect the privacy of
> people who did not ask to be in a book.*
>
> A parent on our school site team asked, at one of our early
> meetings, why the public library was where it was.
>
> The question sounds simple. The branch library closest to MS 223
> was, in those years, a fifteen minute walk from most of our
> children's apartments, and that fifteen minute walk crossed two
> streets where parents did not, after dark, want their children
> walking alone. The library closed at 5:30 in the winter. The
> parent's question, when she asked it, was not really about
> geography. It was about whether the institutions that were
> supposed to be available to our children were, in practice,
> available to them.
>
> The question sat in the room for a while. I do not think any of
> us had a good answer. What I had, in the back of my mind, was the
> bodega.
>
> The bodega in our neighborhood was open from six in the morning
> until midnight. It sold milk and lottery tickets and Tylenol and
> phone chargers. It was, on most blocks in Mott Haven, the most
> reliably open business within walking distance of any apartment in
> its catchment. The shopkeepers were, mostly, neighbors. They knew
> our kids by name. They cashed our parents' checks. They were, in
> many practical respects, more present in our families' daily lives
> than the public library was.
>
> The Community Reading Initiative, which we built over the next
> couple of years, asked a simple question. What if the bodega were
> a branch library?
>
> We worked with merchants who were willing to host a small shelf, a
> donated set of books in English and Spanish, a sign in sheet, and
> a return basket. Children could check a book out of a bodega. The
> librarian, in effect, was the shopkeeper, who knew who had taken
> what and was honest about asking for it back. The books cycled
> through the neighborhood. The bodega became, three or four
> evenings a week, the third place a child could go to find
> something to read.
>
> I am not going to claim that the program turned around anybody's
> reading scores. The honest answer is that we did not have the
> evaluation infrastructure to make that claim, and a coach worth
> her fee would have called me on the carpet for trying.
>
> What the program did, which is what I learned from it, was teach
> the rest of the staff what equity centered design actually
> required. It required us to start from the conditions our
> families were actually living in, not from the conditions a planner
> in 1962 had assumed they would be living in. It required us to
> treat our parents as collaborators with operational knowledge, not
> as recipients of services we had decided in advance they needed.
> And it required us to build relationships, slowly, with merchants
> and faith leaders and beauty shop owners and the people who ran the
> domino tables on the corner, because those were the people who
> knew, in real time, what was actually going on with our families.
>
> A coach helped me see, much later, that the bodega library was the
> moment we had stopped behaving like an institution and started
> behaving like a neighbor. That is the equity move. It is not the
> only equity move. It is one I would recommend to almost any
> principal trying to ground her practice in the lives of the
> children she is supposed to be the guarantor of.
>
> The library is still there. So, for that matter, are most of the
> bodegas. The neighborhood, in some ways, is barely the same
> neighborhood. In the way that mattered to us, it always was.
