# Chapter 1: Mayor of a Six-Block Republic

It is 7:42 on a Tuesday.

The principal has been in her office for forty one minutes. In that
time she has signed two purchase orders, rewritten a press release
about an event that no longer has a venue, answered a parent who
wanted to know, urgently, whether it was true her daughter had been
called a slur by another child in the cafeteria yesterday (it was),
and taken a call from the district office asking whether she could
attend a task force meeting on the superintendent's calendar this
coming Thursday (she could not). The ninth grade dean is outside her
door. A teacher is crying in the nurse's office, though it is not
yet clear why. A boiler somewhere in the basement is making a sound
that the custodian is describing on his radio as *not great, boss.*

The first bell has not rung.

The principal is the only person in the building whose job it is to
know, at that moment, which of these five situations matters most.
That is the job.

## What the metaphor is for

I have been saying, in public, for a long time, that the best
principals are like mayors of small communities (BU Wheelock, 2019).
I say it because I need a metaphor that conveys the scale and the
sound of the job, and because *principal* has become, in American
education, a word that does not do enough work.

A mayor of a small city has to know her police chief and her public
works supervisor and her school board and her senior center director
and her parks and recreation team and her zoning board and her
budget director, and she has to know them well enough to catch the
moment when one of them is telling her a story that is not true. A
mayor does not teach anyone to lay pavement. A mayor does not write
the zoning code herself. She hires the people who do those things
and she holds them accountable and she goes to the ribbon cutting
and she answers the phone when a constituent calls at eleven forty
at night because her husband is sick and the heat on their block is
out.

Swap *teacher* for *police chief.* Swap *school nurse* for *parks
and recreation.* Swap *eighth grade science* for *zoning.* That is
the principal's week.

I did not invent this comparison. I have said it so many times that
some people assume I did. What I like about it is that it forces us
to stop pretending the principal is an administrator with a
background in curriculum. The principal is the mayor of a republic
of five hundred children and forty adults and two thousand parents,
and that republic is six blocks wide. It has an economy, a justice
system, a health code, a language policy, and, on the corner, a
bodega that has opinions about it.

We did not train anyone for this job. We trained them for a smaller
one.

## What the job used to be

The principal in 1977, when I was in middle school, was a man in a
tie who kept the building clean and the buses on time. He was
chosen because he had been a decent teacher and had not made too
many enemies. He kept a master schedule in a binder on his desk. He
went to a conference in Atlantic City once a year. He retired with
a pension, a plaque, and a photograph of himself with three
generations of students he could mostly still name.

That man was doing a job that had been essentially unchanged since
the 1920s, which is when the American secondary school took the
shape we still recognize (Grissom et al., 2021). The principal ran
the operation. Teachers ran the classrooms. A superintendent,
somewhere downtown, ran the district. Curriculum came in a box from
the state. Accountability meant not being in the newspaper.

Nothing about that job required coaching. It required a clipboard.

I do not say this to mock the man in the tie. He did honorable work
inside the expectations he had been given. The expectations were
about to change on him, and on the generation of principals who came
after him, and on me.

## The shift nobody handled well

Somewhere between 1983, which was the year the *A Nation at Risk*
report landed on the national conscience like a rock through a
window, and 2015, which was the year I think we can reasonably say
the shift was complete, the American principalship became three jobs
stacked on top of each other. Nobody announced this out loud. Nobody
redesigned the preparation program in response. Nobody adjusted the
salary.

The first job, the old one, is still there. Run the building. Keep
the buses on time. Close out payroll. Fill the vacancies. Make sure
the fire alarm works. This job is not glamorous, and the people who
tell you they went into education to do this job are, mostly, lying,
but the job still has to be done. If you cannot do the operational
job, you cannot keep the other two.

The second job arrived in the 1990s, quietly at first, then all at
once. Instructional leadership. The principal was suddenly supposed
to know what good teaching looked like. Not in a general, I was a
teacher once, way. In a specific, I can watch a lesson and tell you
three things this teacher needs to change before Friday way. For
most principals of my generation, this was not the job they had
signed up for. The skill was not in their preparation. Their
supervisors, who were themselves former principals, had not been
trained in it either. We learned it on the job, or we did not learn
it, and many of us did not.

The third job showed up around 2005, and it did not go away.
Transformational leadership. The principal was now expected to
redesign the school. Not run it, redesign it. Close the achievement
gap. Build a culture of high expectations. Rewrite the discipline
code so that it stopped expelling children of color at three times
the rate of white children. Launch a dual language program, or a
computer science program, or a restorative justice program, or all
three, on a budget that had been cut in two of the last five years.
This was the job that broke most of my colleagues. It was the job
that made coaching, as a field, necessary.

Three jobs. One salary. One human being.

This is where the mayor metaphor stops being a cute rhetorical move
and starts being, I think, a literal description of what is
expected. A mayor also runs three jobs at once. Nobody pretends a
mayor does it without a chief of staff, a press secretary, a
director of operations, a communications team, and at least one
person whose entire function is to keep her calendar from
collapsing. American principals, most of them, do it with one
secretary and an assistant principal who is usually the person they
trained to replace them.

## Why professional development broke

The response of the profession, to the three job problem, was
professional development.

Go to a conference. Go to a summer institute. Come back with a
three ring binder. Implement what you learned.

This worked, sort of, for a job that was eighty percent operational.
You could, in a three day workshop, learn a better way to do the
master schedule. You could bring back a technique for running a
faculty meeting. You could upgrade your skills inside a job whose
shape stayed still.

It did not work, and it still does not work, for a job whose shape
changes under you in real time. A three day workshop on
instructional leadership does not teach you how to watch a lesson on
Monday and tell the teacher, on Tuesday, that she has been asking
only closed ended questions for three years and her students have
stopped expecting to think. A summer institute on transformational
leadership does not teach you how to tell a veteran social studies
teacher, who has been at the school longer than you have been alive,
that the curriculum he has been teaching for a decade does not, in
fact, include any Black authors. Those are not workshop problems.
They are coaching problems.

The research community took a long time to say this out loud. When
it finally did, mostly through the work of Anthony Bryk and his
colleagues at the Carnegie Foundation, it said it in language that
sounded like engineering (Bryk et al., 2015). The problem with
workshop based PD, Bryk wrote, was that it tried to build
improvement through the logic of product development. Design a
thing. Roll it out at scale. Hope it works. Improvement, he argued,
had to be built the way you build a well run hospital. Small cycles.
Quick feedback. Real time adjustment. Coaching, although he did not
always call it that, was the grammar of the improvement he was
describing.

By then, most of us who had been in the job a while had already
figured out, in our own buildings, that the workshop model had run
out of runway.

## What coaching actually addresses

Coaching is not a replacement for professional development. I am not
going to argue for abolishing the summer institute, which can still
serve a purpose, or for canceling the conferences, which serve a
second purpose that has more to do with loneliness than content
(more on that in Chapter 2). I am going to argue that coaching is
the only intervention structurally shaped to fit a job that is three
jobs stacked on top of each other.

Here is the shape I mean.

A coaching relationship, done right, meets weekly. It is
conversational. It works on the problem the coachee is actually
facing, this week, in her actual building. It gives her a second set
of eyes, which is the single most valuable thing a person doing
three jobs at once can have. It does not require her to extract
herself from the work. It goes to her. It sits in her office. It
watches her run a meeting and then asks her, afterward, what she
noticed.

A workshop cannot do any of that. A workshop is a curriculum looking
for a problem. Coaching is a problem looking for a method.

I will spend the next five chapters walking through the methods. I
want to be honest with you about something first, which is that the
methods matter less than the fit between the coach and the coachee.
The most elegant framework in the world, badly matched, will not
move a principal. A modest framework, well matched and stubbornly
applied, will move a principal a long way. If you remember nothing
else from Part Two of this book, remember that. I will repeat it,
for emphasis, in Chapter 6, when I walk you through what the RAND
Corporation's randomized controlled trials of the NISL program
actually found about coach proximity and coach dosage (Herman et
al., 2020).

## What this book will not fix

I want to say, one more time, before this chapter closes, what
coaching will not do.

Coaching will not fix a principal who has no business being a
principal. There are some, and the most loving thing a coach can do
for one of them is to help her see that the building would be
better off without her in it, and that she would be better off in a
role that was not going to destroy her. I have had that conversation
more than once. It is not pleasant. It is not, strictly, coaching.
It is the thing that sits next to coaching and has to happen
sometimes.

Coaching will not fix a district that has structurally underpaid,
underresourced, and undersupported its principals for twenty years.
That is a policy problem, and coaching addresses it about the same
way a wet towel addresses a house fire. There is a chapter on this
later in the book, Chapter 15, and what it says, in summary, is that
you can coach individual principals out of a burning building but
you cannot coach the building out of being on fire.

Coaching will not rescue you from having to make hard decisions
about who works in your school. A principal who cannot move an
adult, who cannot counsel an underperforming teacher either into
growth or out of the profession, is not going to be rescued by
having a coach who tells her that her feelings about confrontation
are valid. Her feelings are valid. The child in the third row of
that classroom is also valid. Coaching does not let you avoid
choosing between them.

Coaching, when it works, is what makes those choices survivable.
That is a strong claim. I stand by it.

> **Use Case · The Monday Morning Problem**
>
> *A composite, drawn from the experience of several first year
> principals the author has coached. Names, places, and identifying
> details are changed. Nothing in this account is fabricated; all of
> it is disguised.*
>
> She had been the principal for eight days.
>
> The building had been failing, in a slow way that took a long time
> to be admitted to, for nine years. The previous principal had been
> a decent man who retired a year too late. The superintendent had
> hired her, at forty one, because she had been the best assistant
> principal in the district and because the optics of her selection
> (the first woman of color to run that building) were good. She was
> qualified. She was also alone.
>
> On her second Monday, three things happened before eight in the
> morning.
>
> A seventh grade girl came to the nurse with bruises she would not
> explain. The boiler, the one her predecessor had promised her was
> fine, died. And the chair of the PTA, who had not liked her
> selection and had been looking for a pretext, sent an email to the
> school board accusing her of mishandling a field trip permission
> slip. The email had seventeen recipients. Three of them called her
> office within an hour.
>
> She did what a principal with her preparation had been trained to
> do. She triaged. She called child protective services. She called
> the custodian's supervisor. She drafted a reply to the PTA chair
> that she did not send. She sat at her desk at nine fifteen, having
> handled all three situations adequately, and thought, *I cannot do
> this for thirty years.*
>
> The call I got from her that afternoon was the call a coach gets.
> She did not need help triaging the morning. She had triaged the
> morning. She needed help with the feeling that had landed on her
> after the triage was done. The feeling that the three situations
> were not, actually, three situations. They were a pattern. And she
> was the person, now, responsible for whether that pattern kept
> repeating.
>
> I did not offer her a five step model for managing overwhelm. I
> did not send her an article. I asked her one question, which was
> the question I have asked dozens of first year principals in
> similar moments.
>
> *Which of those three problems*, I asked her, *would you still be
> having if the previous principal had done his job?*
>
> She was quiet for a long time. Then she said, *all of them.*
>
> *Good*, I said. *Now we know what we are actually working on.*
>
> What we were actually working on, it turned out, was not the
> Monday morning problem. It was a nine year accumulation of
> decisions that had never been made, relationships that had been
> allowed to calcify, and expectations that had been allowed to
> collapse. The three situations that morning were symptoms. She had
> been hired, whether the hiring committee knew it or not, to be the
> person who finally treated the disease.
>
> That is what coaching was for. Not the triage. The diagnosis.
>
> She is, as of this writing, in her ninth year as a principal. The
> building is not fixed. It is, in her words, *no longer actively on
> fire*. She coaches two other new principals in her district now.
> She is planning to go for a superintendency in another five years.
> I have told her she is ready earlier than she thinks. We will
> argue about that, on a weekly call, for a while.
